## Headline verdict

**An on-switch outside the operator, wired to an acceptance still inside it. Binding in law, paperwork in crisis time.**

The Act is the corpus's first binding instrument and its first external on-switch, and it has two faces. From the operator's seat it is an enforcement regime; from a frontier lab's seat it is a regime that sees into the training run, holds a source-code door and an armed withdrawal power over the model, and offers no approved code to shelter under. Both readings are text-true, because binding force is distributed by role and tier.

For the systems in critical infrastructure, the proposition that survived the seven stops reads: nothing in Chapter III binds until December 2027, and after that what stands between a high-risk system and the market is the maker's own signature, unless a sectoral notified body puts an outside eye in first; the standards route adds a presumption, not an eye. External judgement with legal force comes only after the event. The installed base never enters Chapter III unless its design changes significantly, public-authority systems excepted, which must comply by 2 August 2030.

In the analyst's terms: the gap the lab frameworks leave at the deployment layer moved from the labs' silence to the maker's label, with the legislature's list behind it (Article 3(12); Article 6(3)); and in the moment the authority issues orders and does not touch the plant, the hand on the process is the operator's, which is as it should be.

## Scope

Two units, two attachment events. An AI system is caught by product law (Article 6(1): a safety component of an Annex I product that needs third-party conformity assessment) or by listed use (Article 6(2) and Annex III), at the moment it is placed on the market or put into service, including "for own use" (Article 3(11)), which makes a plant that builds its own tool provider and deployer at once. A general-purpose model is caught by capability class, the compute presumption at 10^25 FLOP (Article 51(2)) or designation, and only the Commission can keep it out or let it out (Article 52). Pre-market research and development are outside (Article 2(8), Article 3(63)); the Act sees the training run because it is told about it (Article 52(1), two weeks) and does not hold it: sight, not reach.

The hold changes hands at the sectoral rules for machinery and transport, where Annex I Section B systems get only Article 6(1), Article 60a and Articles 102 to 112 (Article 2(2)) and the requirements travel into the neighbouring acts; it runs out at the national-security exclusion (Article 2(3)). It stops short of some of the critical infrastructure the analyst has worked in; how much depends on two words in Annex III point 2, on Article 6(1b), and on a comparison stated under Open questions.

The Act binds the deployed-system layer, the first framework in the corpus to do so, inside a perimeter drawn by use and product; outside it the layer is open by statute, and a systemic-risk model in an unlisted use carries Article 55 duties and no deployment-tier duty. The gap is relocated from the labs' silence to the legislature's list, amendable (Article 7) and reviewed annually (Article 112(1)); not closed, not merely reproduced. Article 2(1)(c) closes the offshore route: output used in the Union brings the provider in.

## Risk approach

Three regimes under one title, each with its own unit, clocks and enforcer: a short list of prohibitions (Article 5); a use-tiered regime for high-risk systems (Chapter III); a capability-tiered regime for general-purpose models (Chapter V).

The high-risk regime is a bounded, self-accepted risk-management system. Article 9(3) confines the universe to risks that "may be reasonably mitigated or eliminated" by design or by information; Article 9(5) requires residual risk to be "judged to be acceptable", no number, no threshold, no judge named, the provider by implication of Article 16(a). That is entry seven on the corpus's residual-risk spectrum: self-acceptor, unnamed, attested and lodged; re-judged externally only after the event. The model tier has no acceptance act in the statute at all; Article 55(1)(b) assesses and mitigates and never accepts, and the acceptance is delegated to the Code (Thread B's entry eight).

The obligations fall into the corpus's three bins: a failable shell of documentary and clock-bound duties around a self-referential centre ("acceptable", "appropriate and proportionate", "an appropriate level"), with two exits the voluntary instruments lack, a criterion by presumption once a harmonised standard is cited (Article 40) and a re-judge with force after the event. Unfailable before market, failable after it. The same words carry opposite failability: "reasonably foreseeable misuse" is a matter for the provider's own assessment under Article 9(2)(b) and an outcome the state tests with a 7 percent fine under Article 5(1a).

The incident definition (Article 3(49)) has four heads and no pre-harm tier: "hazard" appears once in the enacting terms, "near miss" never. For critical infrastructure the threshold is a "serious and irreversible disruption" (head (b)), so for critical infrastructure the Act learns only from the disruptions nobody could reverse. Recovery is a species null: the product-safety chassis withdraws and recalls and has never restored service; "recover" appears once, in a cost clause; restoration duties live next door in the CER and NIS2 directives, which the Act touches only to borrow a definition (Article 3(62)) and, for incident reporting, to yield to (Article 73(9)). A silent seam, not a hand-off.

## Enforcement model

Seven switches, by holder and clock. The legislature's, Article 5, thrown once in statute, needing no hand. The deployer's, Article 26(5), "shall suspend the use of that system" on its own reason to consider a risk, no clock, no one to ask, its own use only: the one stop that works in crisis time, and a duty. The provider's, Article 20(1), "immediately" correct, withdraw, disable or recall, the only verb that runs across a fleet. The national authority's on a system, Article 79 with 82: an evaluation on "sufficient reason to consider", an order to the operator within a period the authority sets under a fifteen-working-day ceiling, and the state's own hand on the system only after the operator has failed (79(5)), then a three-month objection window (thirty days for Article 5) and the safeguard procedure (81).

The AI Office's on an Article 75(1) system, the same powers plus a decision route and periodic penalties up to 5 percent of average daily worldwide turnover (75c). The Commission's on a model, Article 93(1)(c): a request, where necessary and appropriate, no evaluation as precondition, no clock, executed by the lab's own hand under Article 101, with structured dialogue and binding commitments as the off-ramp. And the state's on the machinery itself, Articles 46 and 5(3), which switch conformity off rather than the system: an unassessed system may run on a reasoned request in advance, open to anyone, "key industrial and infrastructural assets" a named ground; act-first only for law-enforcement and civil-protection authorities; refusal means "stopped with immediate effect". A private operator can never act first.

The verdict on the corpus's first external on-switch: an on-switch in law, a paperwork switch in crisis time. In the moment the state holds no stop of its own before the operator has failed, and none on a crisis clock; what it holds in the moment is the reverse power. The stops that work are the operator's and the provider's. The trigger determination sits outside the operator's building for the first time in the corpus, twice (the MSA against a system, the Commission against a model); the crisis-time switches are internal, self-judged, and obligatory. Against the voluntary instruments the statutory switch is standing and slow where the ad hoc state instances of June 2026 were fast and outside any statute.

Verb census over the enacting terms and annexes, counts in the reasoning note: "delay" as a stop verb is absent, "halt" appears once as the safe-state stop button of Article 14(4)(e), and the statute's own verbs are suspend, disable, withdraw, recall, restrict and "stopped with immediate effect". Penalties: EUR 35 million or 7 percent for a prohibited practice; EUR 15 million or 3 percent for operator obligations and for model providers; EUR 7.5 million or 1 percent for misleading information. None levied as of 13 September 2026.

Enforcement follows the provider. Article 75(1) gives the AI Office exclusive competence over the labs' own model-plus-system products, applies it to providers only and to deployers only within the same undertaking, and carves out Annex I products and Annex III point 2 systems: for a control-room tool on a lab's model, the model is the AI Office's, the tool and its operator are the national authority's.

## Who it binds

Providers, by tier: model providers under Chapter V by capability (Article 53 for all; Article 55 for systemic risk); system providers under Chapter III by use or product, from December 2027 and August 2028. Deployers of high-risk systems, thinly but failably (Article 26; Article 27's fundamental-rights impact assessment for public bodies and some private ones, "with the exception of" Annex III point 2, so a critical-infrastructure deployer has no assessment duty anywhere in the instrument). Everyone, under Article 5, Article 50 and Article 4's obligation of effort. Roles are cumulative and switchable: putting a name on a system, substantially modifying it, or modifying its intended purpose makes an operator the provider (Article 25(1)); off-label use makes the operator the manufacturer, and the labs' terms of use are the label on the box. Provider and deployer are caught independently under Article 5 (Article 5(1a)'s two limbs; Article 99(3) fines "non-compliance with the prohibition" without naming an operator).

The state as customer gets an outside eye before market in some cases (Article 43(1), the market surveillance authority as notified body for police uses) and the Act's only act-first latitude (Articles 5(3), 46(2)), and the only deadline on the installed base, 2 August 2030 for public-authority systems (111(2)), where the private installed base has none. An outside eye before market otherwise exists only where product law already requires a notified body (Article 43(3), the sectoral route) or where the state permits unassessed use (Article 46). On the Annex III route (Article 43(2)) nobody but the maker looks before go-live.

Section B sectors, aviation, rail, marine equipment, vehicles and now machinery (Regulation (EU) 2023/1230, moved by the Omnibus), are bound elsewhere, not outside: the Act's requirements are inserted into their own acts and enforced there. The reverse guardrail fires at this field: the Act binds a deployer at all, which no lab framework does, and it creates duties across the model-to-system seam (Article 53(1)(b) documentation for integrators; Article 25(4) the written agreement with an upstream model supplier) that no voluntary instrument carries.

## Key obligations

Providers of high-risk systems (Articles 9 to 21, 43, 47 to 49, 72, 73): a risk management system, data governance, technical documentation, logging, instructions for use, human oversight built in with a stop or safe-state procedure (14(4)(e)), accuracy, robustness and cybersecurity at "an appropriate level", a quality management system, internal-control conformity assessment for Annex III points 2 to 8 (43(2)), the declaration and CE marking, registration including any Article 6(3) opt-out, post-market monitoring, serious-incident reporting, and corrective action "immediately" on reason to consider non-conformity (20(1)). Deployers of high-risk systems (Article 26): use per instructions, competent and empowered oversight, monitoring and suspension on reason to consider a risk with information to provider and authority, and six months of logs. Providers of general-purpose models (Articles 52, 53, 55): documentation for the AI Office and for integrators, a copyright policy, a public training-content summary, notification within two weeks of meeting or knowing they will meet the systemic-risk threshold, and for systemic risk evaluation "in accordance with standardised protocols and tools reflecting the state of the art", assessment and mitigation, incident reporting to the AI Office "without undue delay", and cybersecurity; no external evaluator is named; codes demonstrate compliance until a standard exists (55(2)), and a provider relying on neither must demonstrate "alternative adequate means" for Commission assessment.

Clocks that bind action: on the reporter, fifteen days from awareness for a serious incident, two days for a widespread infringement or a critical-infrastructure disruption, ten days for a death (73(2) to (4)); on the authority, seven days to act under 2019/1020 Article 19 on receipt of a report (73(8)), the corpus's first response clock on an actor; on the operator, the period the authority sets under 79(2). Tacit clocks where silence acts: thirty days approves a real-world testing plan (60(4)(b)), the fastest clock in the instrument and it runs in the market's favour; fifteen days lets an emergency authorisation stand (46(4)). The model tier carries no clock in either direction.

Two limits on the incident duty. It attaches to providers of high-risk systems (73(1)), so a head (b) disruption at an unlisted facility owes no report under this Act; and inside the list it yields to "Union legislative instruments laying down reporting obligations equivalent" wherever the provider is already subject to them (73(9)), "equivalent" undefined and unassigned. An operator that builds its own tool and is a NIS2 or CER entity reports an in-house system's critical-infrastructure incident next door; only a vendor-supplied high-risk system reports it here on the two-day clock. Before 2 December 2027 the machinery has no system-level subject at all.

## Intersections with crisis / resilience

Under pressure the Act assumes design-time behaviour: that an operator reads the intended purpose and stays inside it (25(1)(c)), and that an emergency runs on the regulator's clock, with time to write a reasoned request and wait for the paper before touching an unassessed tool (46(1)). The analyst's testimony, carried as testimony and not evidence: in a live event nobody in the control room checks a tool's intended purpose; they use whatever gives them an answer they trust, and the paperwork catches up afterwards; he has never seen an operator wait for written authorisation before using a tool in an emergency, and authorisation, where it existed, was sought after the fact. For an Annex III point 2 system the form does not exist to be skipped before December 2027; for the installed base it never exists unless the design changes significantly.

The operational stop belongs to the closest hand: built by the provider (14(4)(e)), staffed by the deployer (26(2)), pulled by the deployer on its own judgement (26(5)). The provider's "disable" runs downhill across a fleet; the deployer's stop does not run uphill; the state sits above both and touches the system only after the operator has failed. No unified command: the Act has coordination duties (74(10) "shall facilitate coordination"; 74(11) joint investigations "may" be proposed) and no command tree, and no authority is designated for critical infrastructure, which gets whichever general authority the Member State names under Article 70. One event in a gas control room can owe three reports to three authorities on three clocks with no named lead: this Act's two days, NIS2's twenty-four-hour early warning and seventy-two-hour notification, CER's twenty-four-hour initial notification (the neighbouring clocks page-checked at EUR-Lex before citation).

The incident grammar starts where recovery has already failed: for critical infrastructure a recoverable outage is not a serious incident, and the near-miss tier that mature incident doctrine runs on does not exist here, as it did not in NIST AI 600-1. Article 73(6) settles scene against restoration in favour of the scene: no investigation that alters the system in a way that may affect evaluation of the causes "prior to informing the competent authorities". Testimony beside it: in a plant you restore first and preserve what you can; a rule that says tell the authority before you alter the system would be honoured in the breach in any live event the analyst has been part of.

The safety case is missing from both sides, and the post-Piper Alpha pattern, regulator accepts before operation, is not what this law does for the systems in the analyst's world: nobody outside the maker accepts the case before operation on the Annex III route, and the critical-infrastructure deployer never makes one for its own plant, Article 27 carving point 2 out of the only deployer-side assessment the Act has.

Two direct reaches into crisis tooling: emergency-call evaluation, dispatch, prioritisation and triage systems are high-risk (Annex III point 5(d)); and Article 50 bites on a crisis manager's communications, a public-information chatbot must disclose itself unless obvious (50(1)), AI-drafted text published to inform the public on matters of public interest must be disclosed unless it has passed human editorial control (50(4)), and emotion recognition or biometric categorisation must be disclosed to those exposed (50(3)).

## Open questions

1. Does "supply of gas" in Annex III point 2 reach upstream gas and LNG operations, or only the utility chain? Two hinges, both open, not-a-lawyer. First, the words themselves: the legislature mapped "critical digital infrastructure" to the CER Annex and left "the supply of water, gas, heating and electricity" as plain words mapped to nothing (2024 recital 55). Second, "safety component": the protective layer, the trip, the alarm and the shutdown, is inside, and the tool that runs or optimises the operation is not (recital 55 with Articles 6(1a) and 6(1b); the gloss is in the reasoning note).
2. Does 2019/1020's rapid-intervention lane (its Article 19), invoked by name only at Article 73(8), give a national authority a hand on an AI system faster than Article 79's sequence when no report has been made?
3. Who decides that another Union instrument's reporting obligations are "equivalent" under Article 73(9), and against what test?
4. What counts as "significant changes in their designs" under Article 111(2), a term the Act does not define (Article 3(23) defines "substantial modification", a different term), so the provider decides when its own installed system crosses into Chapter III; a candidate reading, not ruled.
5. Three articles (53(4), 55(2), 89(1)) still refer to "an approved code of practice" after the Omnibus removed the approval power and left a published adequacy assessment; whether the assessment does the work of "approved" is Thread B's first question.
6. Does the Code define a model-level serious incident, since 3(49) is written for systems and 55(1)(c) applies the term to models with no definition and no clock? Thread B.
7. Will the outcome-level harmonised standards for Articles 9 to 15 be cited before 2 December 2027? The first standard published, EN 18286:2026, is a quality management system for Article 17, not yet cited; the outcome deliverables are in the CEN-CENELEC programme with dates that have slipped four times. Sequencing with slippage as the watch.
8. Whether the Commission's stated fastest response to "an imminent threat of large-scale irreversible harm", to "consider whether rapid guidance ... or a rapid update to the Code" is appropriate (Service Desk FAQ, verified 13 September 2026), ever acquires a clock or a procedure. Thread B.
